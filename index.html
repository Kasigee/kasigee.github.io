About

Understanding the molecular processes behind our own biologysolar-panel recyclingthe Hofmeister seriestextbook chemistrycryopreservationlight harvestingour biology, industry and the fundamentals of chemistry

I'm a computational chemist and Lecturer in Chemistry at the University of New England, and a Senior Lecturer from January 2027. My work asks how molecules behave in the processes that matter: the ones that underpin our own biology, the ones industry depends on, like freeing the materials locked in a retired solar panel, and the fundamental concepts chemistry is taught with.

AI runs through that work. Machine-learned potentials let me simulate bigger systems for longer than quantum chemistry alone allows, and AI tools speed up much of the rest. I check every model against high-level quantum chemistry and experiment. That checking cuts both ways: it tells me what the molecules are doing, and it shows where AI models do and don't understand the chemistry.

My PhD at the University of Newcastle (2022) used quantum chemistry to explain Hofmeister effects in non-aqueous solvents. As a postdoctoral fellow at the Australian National University, I built a maximum bubble pressure tensiometer to measure non-aqueous electrolytes, then used molecular dynamics to study salt thermodiffusion for membrane-free desalination and transport in the malaria parasite.

Solar-panel recycling is my newest project, not my only one. This year's work runs from a transporter in the malaria parasite to how organic chemistry is taught.

Lead project · University of New England

Using AI to take old solar panels apart

Australia expects about a million tonnes of retired solar panels by 2035. Most of a panel's value, its silicon, silver and copper, sits under a tough plastic glue built to hold the panel together for 25 to 30 years. Releasing it cleanly and cheaply is the first step in recycling.

I lead the research. I designed the simulation workflow and run it on NCI's Gadi supercomputer under a national computing allocation I lead, using machine-learned interatomic potentials trained on quantum chemistry. They make large molecular dynamics simulations fast enough to screen solvents far more quickly than trial and error in the lab. I check every model against high-level quantum chemistry, and every screen against laboratory results. Lab testing at UNE comes next.

In the press: pv magazine (May 2026) · Sustainability Matters (May 2026) · Lab Next 70 (November 2025)

Fundamental concepts

Rethinking textbook organic chemistry

Our 2025 Chemical Science paper found that the inductive effect doesn't explain the electron density in haloacetates, a classic textbook example. This year, three papers and a published reply in the Journal of Chemical Education rethink how inductive effects, hyperconjugation and the reactivity of aldehydes and ketones are taught.

“Rethinking the nature and extent of inductive effects” was among the ten most-read ACS journal articles in May 2026, and was covered by C&EN, Chemistry World, Phys.org, ScienceDaily and Mirage News.

Molecules in living systems

Led by Ciara Wallis, with Ben Corry's group at ANU: a proton-transfer mechanism for the malaria parasite's lactate/H⁺ symporter, a target for new antimalarials. It turns out to work like a channel, without the large conformational changes most transporters rely on. I'm on Ciara's PhD supervisory panel.

Molecular dynamics in your browser

Your browser is integrating Newton's equations of motion for 216 Lennard-Jones atoms in a periodic box, in time steps of 0.005 τ (about 11 fs for argon).

Turn the thermostat down and the gas condenses into droplets. Turn it up and the speed distribution broadens, following the Maxwell–Boltzmann curve for the measured temperature.

My research simulations work the same way, with far more atoms and with forces from quantum chemistry or machine-learned potentials, on the Gadi supercomputer.

Chemical Science · 2021 · first author · front cover

Quantifying the Hofmeister series

Salts change how proteins fold, how polymers swell and how surfaces interact, in a consistent order known as the Hofmeister series. The order has been known for over a century, but explaining it has been hard.

In Chemical Science we traced the anion series to electrostatics at each ion's most charge-dense atom, which reduces every anion to one number: its radial charge density. Our review in Physical Chemistry Chemical Physics, now Web of Science Highly Cited, sets out what is and isn't understood about specific ion effects.

Scientific Data · 2022 · first author

IonSolvR: a repository of solvated ions

How an ion holds its shell of solvent shapes everything from battery electrolytes to protein stability. Studying ions outside water has been held back by a lack of consistent data.

IonSolvR is an open collection of more than 3,000 nanosecond-scale quantum chemical (DFTB) molecular dynamics simulations, covering up to 55 solutes in 28 protic and aprotic solvents. Every run uses one protocol and is validated against experiment, so others can build on it instead of starting from scratch.

Journal of Computational Chemistry · 2025 · corresponding author

Predicting PAH stability from geometry

Curving a polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon costs energy, but computing that cost with high-level quantum chemistry for tens of thousands of isomers is slow.

We computed PBE0-D4 energies for 38,264 nonplanar PAH isomers and tested which cheap geometric descriptor tracks them. The best is Σdihedral, the sum of the deviations from planarity across every C–C–C–C dihedral. It predicts relative stability to a mean absolute deviation of 3.6 kcal mol⁻¹, and 0.8 kcal mol⁻¹ when combined with aromaticity and fitted GFN2-xTB energies. We released the PAHAPS web tool (PAH Automated Property Scanner) and code with the paper.

Teaching

Teaching AI for science

I developed and coordinate SCI310/510, Artificial Intelligence and Computing for Science, at UNE, and teach first-year, physical and quantum chemistry. I coordinate first-year teaching and learning for the School of Science and Technology, and have built computational chemistry labs and teaching materials since 2016.

AI in practice

Using AI well in research

I run workshops on using AI in research, from strategy and workflows to ethics, economics and e-safety, for UNE's Graduate Research School and other disciplines. In my own research I use AI coding assistants every day, and machine-learned potentials wherever quantum chemistry alone would be too slow.
