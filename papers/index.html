Chemical Science · 2021 · first author

The Hofmeister series follows charge per unit radius

Hofmeister effects, where different salts change protein stability, viscosity or reaction rates in a consistent order, have resisted a quantitative explanation for over a century. For anions, this paper traces them to Coulomb interactions at the ion's most charge-dense atom. It reduces each anion to one number, the radial charge density ϸ = q/r: that atom's partial charge divided by its effective radius.

The surface is ϸ = q/r itself. Each sphere sits at an anion's computed site charge and radius, so its height is its place in the series. The white lines are constant ϸ: anions on the same line should behave alike, even when their size and charge differ.

    Understanding specific ion effects and the Hofmeister series

    Different salts change protein stability, polymer swelling, surface tension and reaction rates in a consistent order, known as the Hofmeister series. More than a century of work has produced many explanations, but no agreed one.

    This review, written with experimental and computational colleagues, sets out what is and isn't understood about specific ion effects and the Hofmeister series.

    Scientific Data · 2022 · first author

    IonSolvR: more than 3,000 quantum MD runs of ions in solvent

    Studying ions outside water has been limited by a lack of consistent data. IonSolvR is an open repository of nanosecond-scale density functional tight binding (DFTB3-D3(BJ)) molecular dynamics trajectories, covering up to 55 ionic solutes in 28 protic and aprotic solvents, all run with one protocol.

    Effective concentration is set by cell size. The scene shows the four NaCl–water cells described in the paper, to scale. The water positions are a random packing drawn for this page, like the random starting boxes the simulations began from; they are not frames from the repository.

    Chemical Science · 2025 · led by E. C. Johnson

    Are our textbooks wrong about the inductive effect?

    The inductive effect, the idea that an electronegative atom pulls electron density along a chain of bonds, is one of the first explanations organic chemistry students learn. Haloacetates are the textbook example: change the halogen and the acid's strength changes.

    This 2025 Chemical Science paper, led by E. C. Johnson, found that the inductive effect doesn't explain the electron density in haloacetates. Four follow-ups in the Journal of Chemical Education rethink how inductive effects, hyperconjugation and the reactivity of aldehydes and ketones are taught. Each one is on the shelf too:

    Journal of Computational Chemistry · 2025 · corresponding author

    Geometry alone goes a long way toward predicting PAH stability

    Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons matter for pollution toxicity and for carbon materials, and their isomers differ widely in stability. This paper computed PBE0-D4 energies for 38,264 nonplanar PAH isomers, a method that matches CCSD(T) to 0.67 kcal mol⁻¹ on a 335-molecule benchmark. It then asked which cheap geometric measure tracks those energies.

    The winner is Σdihedral, the sum of the deviations from planarity across every C–C–C–C dihedral. It costs nothing to compute from coordinates, and the paper's PAHAPS web tool uses it to estimate an isomer's energy without a quantum calculation.

    bioRxiv · 2026 · preprint · led by Ciara Wallis

    How the malaria parasite's lactate transporter moves protons

    Malaria parasites run on glycolysis, and must export the lactate it produces, together with a proton, through a transporter in their membrane. That transporter is a target for new antimalarial drugs.

    Ciara Wallis led this work as first author, with Ben Corry's group at the Australian National University; I'm on her PhD supervisory panel. It proposes a proton-transfer mechanism, and points to a channel-like transporter that works without the large conformational changes most transporters rely on.

    Wallis, Gregory, Fairweather, van Dooren, Lehane and Corry, “A proton transfer mechanism in the malaria parasite lactate/H⁺ symporter reveals a channel-like transporter without conformational changes”, bioRxiv (2026)
